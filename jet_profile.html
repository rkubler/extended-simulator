Jet profile across the line

The jet profile describes how the shots are spread across the width of the jet: what share of the shots lands at each distance y from the jet axis. It decides whether neighbouring lines of a raster blend into an even treatment or leave stripes.

Both curves enclose the same area (∫ g dy = 1): the same number of shots, spread differently. The Gaussian jet is 1.6 times denser on its axis, falls to 13.5 % of its peak at the edges of ΔX, and puts 4.6 % of the shots just outside ΔX.

What the profile does to neighbouring lines

The sum Σ g(y − jδ) gives how many shots each point receives from all the lines of a raster. It is shown here relative to its mean 1/δ: a flat line at 1 means an even treatment, waves mean stripes.

  • Gaussian jet: the lines blend smoothly. Stripes stay below 3 % as long as δ ≤ 2σⱼ = ΔX/2, and grow quickly beyond.
  • Uniform jet: even only when ΔX/δ is a whole number (δ = ΔX, ΔX/2, ΔX/3…). Otherwise sharp steps; for δ > ΔX, untreated gaps.

Why the two curves can be in opposition. The Gaussian jet always has its maxima on the lines, where its dense centre passes. The uniform jet counts how many bands of width ΔX overlap at each point: on a line it receives 2⌊m/2⌋ + 1 bands and halfway between two lines 2⌊(m+1)/2⌋ bands, with m = ΔX/δ. For 1 < m < 2 (for example δ = 15 mm with ΔX = 20 mm), a point on a line is reached by one band but a point between two lines by two: the uniform maxima fall between the lines, opposite to the Gaussian ones. For 2 < m < 3 (for example δ = 8 mm) the maxima are on the lines for both, then they alternate again for 3 < m < 4, and so on. This is a real effect of the top-hat shape, not a display error.

σⱼ = ΔX/4 is a modelling convention, not a measurement. The real profile of a nozzle can be measured by scanning the coverage or the Almen intensity across a single stationary sweep.
